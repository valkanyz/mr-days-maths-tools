    How the model works & its limits

    This is an accelerated, simplified model of sexual reproduction in imaginary beetles. Every individual has 12 independent diploid loci (24 allele copies). Trait value is the proportion of “1” alleles, displayed on a 0–100 scale. Colour, body proportions and antenna length are linked visual representations of this single trait. Low values are amber and round with short antennae; high values are violet and slender with long antennae. Offspring receive one randomly sampled allele per locus from each parent; mutations flip alleles independently, without regard to what the habitat needs.

    Each generation exchanges migrants if connected, then replaces the adults with offspring. Parents closer to the local optimum have greater expected reproductive contribution. Relative fitness = exp(−18 × selection × (trait − optimum)²), with trait and optimum on a 0–1 scale. The two parents must be distinct and within the selected trait tolerance. Each habitat retains its chosen population size: density regulation is assumed, so this is not an extinction or population-growth model. Positions on the map are illustrative, not movement tracks.

    Random inheritance and parent sampling produce genetic drift. The barrier stops migration; it does not directly change genes or mate compatibility. Mate choice based on the ecological trait is an explicit modelling assumption. No hybrid infertility, incompatible chromosomes, sex differences or evolution of mate preference are simulated. All compatible crosses produce viable, fertile offspring.

    The indicator compares cross-population compatibility with average within-population compatibility. Less than 10% of that baseline for 10 consecutive generations is labelled “strong reproductive isolation in this model”. That is an illustrative threshold, not a universal definition or proof of a real new species. Real speciation can involve many mechanisms and very different timescales. Altering mate tolerance changes the assumed breeding rule immediately; it is not an evolved change.

    Migration uses random reciprocal exchanges to preserve habitat sizes. The slider is a target fraction; the actual exchanged count varies. CSV exports include settings for each recorded generation. A separate random stream is used for mating tests so tests do not alter the evolutionary trajectory.
